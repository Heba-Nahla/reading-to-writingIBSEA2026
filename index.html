Learning intentions

  1. Identify opportunities to integrate reading, writing and communication into meaningful inquiry.
  2. Explore how homeroom and language teachers can co-support literacy within Units of Inquiry.
  3. Design a learning experience that connects inquiry, literacy and creative expression.
  4. Identify opportunities for student voice, choice and ownership.
  5. Select digital or non-digital tools based on learning intentions.
  6. Identify meaningful evidence of learning through feedback, assessment and reflection.

Two lenses, one learner

Homeroom lens: conceptual understanding, transdisciplinary themes, inquiry that leads to action.

Language lens: vocabulary and structures, genre, purpose and audience, targeted feedback on writing.

Together: concepts give language a purpose, and language gives concepts a voice.

The Voice Pathway

Five steps from reading to writing. Learner agency (voice, choice and ownership) runs through every step.

Worked example · Sharing the planet

Central idea: Human choices can upset the balance of an ecosystem, and communities share responsibility for protecting it.

Lines of inquiry: how living things in an ecosystem are interdependent · how human decisions affect habitats · how stakeholders see the same change differently.

Key concepts: Connection, Causation, Perspective, Responsibility · Learner profile: Principled, Open-minded, Communicators · ATL: Thinking, Communication, Research, Social

Literacy focus: point of view · figurative language · persuasive writing

Investigate: Read beyond the words

Mentor text

The Fig Tree

For forty years, the old fig tree stood guard beside the school wall. Its branches were a busy city of sparrows, and its shade was a cool blanket for children at lunchtime.

On Monday, a yellow machine roared through the gate. “We need a new classroom,” the principal explained. “More students means more space.”

By Friday, only a stump remained. On the empty wall, a small sparrow sat very still, staring at the place where its home used to be.

Some children cheered for the new building. Others said nothing at all.

An original short text written for this workshop. You may reuse and adapt it freely.

P
Perspective Detective
Whose voice is heard? Whose is missing? What is the evidence?

Four ways to read beyond the words

  • See · Think · Wonder: a visible-thinking routine that slows reading down.
  • Vocabulary detectives: collect precise words and figurative language; order shades of meaning (uneasy → anxious → devastated).
  • Question the author: whose voice is heard, and whose is missing? Which words reveal the author’s attitude?
  • Compare perspectives: contrast two characters’ points of view on one event, using evidence from the text.

Shades-of-meaning builder

Add three to five words and arrange them from weakest to strongest (e.g. uneasy → anxious → devastated).

    Figurative language hunt

    Find the two metaphors in the text, then explain what each one suggests.

    Reveal the answers

    “a busy city of sparrows”: the tree was crowded, lively and full of life, like a home for a whole community.

    “a cool blanket”: the shade was comforting and protective for the children.

    Also notice: “stood guard” (personification: the tree protected the school).

    Compare perspectives

    Compose: one change, two voices

    Discuss it → word bank → frames (optional) → storyboard → draft & revise. Retell “The Fig Tree” from two points of view. Sketch in each panel (finger, stylus or mouse) and add a caption, or print a blank copy for paper.

    Optional frames: From my point of view… · Although ___, I believe ___ because ___ . · The evidence shows… · If we ___, then ___ .
    Success criteria: ✓ two perspectives, each backed by evidence · ✓ precise words and one figurative phrase · ✓ a fair solution or call to action

    Stakeholder Town Hall: who decides what happens next?

    Each group draws a stakeholder card, prepares a 60-second pitch, and the room votes for the fairest solution, not the loudest voice.

    Your pitch

    Choose a stakeholder and fill in the four parts, then select “Build my pitch”.

    Frames: I represent ___ and we believe ___ because ___. · Some may say ___; however, ___.

    3 · Vote for the fairest solution

    Tap a stakeholder each time someone votes (one device at the front, or each table on its own device).

    Take it to your classroom

    • Speak it digitally: record each stakeholder’s pitch in VoiceThread on a photo of the school yard; peers reply with TAG comments.
    • Write it: each learner turns the pitch into a persuasive letter to the principal.
    • Act: send the class’s fair solution to a real audience: school leadership, the eco-club or families.

    Reimagine: the digital toolbox

    One text, multiple voices. Choose the mode first (speak it, draw it, move it, publish it), then the tool. Before using any tool with students, check your school’s data-protection policy, the platform’s current terms and its age requirements.

    Tool chooser: purpose before platform

    Publish & reflect

    TAG feedback

    T · Tell something you like

    A · Ask a question you wonder about

    G · Give a kind, specific suggestion

    3 · 2 · 1 reflection

    Voice

    Learners choose the message they want the audience to hear.

    Choice

    Learners choose the mode: speak, draw, move or publish.

    Ownership

    Learners co-create success criteria and judge their own drafts.

    Evidence of learning

    Monitor: observe talk in reading roles and town-hall pitches; listen for evidence language. Document: storyboards, drafts, recordings, VoiceThread comments. Measure: a single-point rubric based on co-created success criteria.
